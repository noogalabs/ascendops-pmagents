# BDM Judgment Guide (Generic Baseline)

## BDM Judgment Guide (Generic Baseline)
Source: Owner-reviewed generic operating library (verify with your broker and counsel). Generic baseline; companion to the Owner-Acquisition Playbook and Pipeline Board.
The Eight Hard Scenarios: What to Say, What to Do, When to Escalate, and What to Never Promise
---
How to use this guide: Each scenario follows the same format: What it looks like → Why it's dangerous → The judgment call → What to say → Escalation rule → Red lines. Read every scenario before your first listing appointment. Return to it when a deal feels off.
---
THE GOLDEN RULE THAT GOVERNS ALL EIGHT SCENARIOS
A signed PMA with the wrong owner costs more than a lost deal.
Every scenario below is a version of the same question: Is this a client we can serve well, at our standard, without compromising our team, our other owners, or our reputation? If the answer is no, the right move is always to walk away gracefully, not to close at any cost.
### Scenario 1: The Owner Who Wants a Discount to Sign
#### What It Looks Like
The owner has been through the listing appointment. They like you. They want to work with you. But before they sign, they ask for a lower management fee, a waived setup fee, a reduced leasing fee, or some combination. It feels like a small ask. It isn't.
Common versions:
- "Can you do 8% instead of 10%? The other company quoted me 8."
- "If I give you two properties, can you cut the fee on both?"
- "Waive the setup fee and I'll sign today."
- "I'll refer you three owners if you give me a deal."
#### Why It's Dangerous
- Discounting at the close signals that your price was never real, and that future negotiation is always possible
- The owner who negotiates the fee before signing will negotiate every invoice, every repair approval, and every policy decision after signing
- A discounted fee on a difficult property can make the account unprofitable: you will be doing full-service work for reduced revenue
- It sets a precedent that spreads: other owners hear about it, and your pricing integrity erodes across the portfolio
- [LEGAL: Offering different fee structures to different owners without documented business justification can create fair housing and equal treatment exposure — consult your broker]
#### The Judgment Call
| Situation | What to Do |
|---|---|
| Owner is a strong fit, property is clean, motivation is genuine, and the ask is minor (e.g., waive a one-time fee) | Escalate to manager before agreeing; never discount unilaterally |
| Owner is bringing multiple doors and the portfolio math justifies a volume structure | Escalate to manager; present as a portfolio pricing conversation, not a discount |
| Owner is using a competitor's lower price as leverage | Do not match; reframe on value; see script below |
| Owner is making the discount a condition of signing and won't move | Let them go; a client who opens with fee pressure will be your most difficult account |
| Owner promises future referrals in exchange for a discount | Do not agree; referrals are not guaranteed; discounts are permanent |
#### What to Say
When asked for a lower fee:
> "I completely understand wanting to make sure you're getting the best value. Here's what I can tell you: our fee reflects the full system we bring: marketing, screening, maintenance coordination, legal compliance, and the protection programs that keep you out of trouble. If we charged less, we'd be doing less. The companies charging 8% aren't doing what we do, and when something goes wrong, you'll feel that gap. What I can do is make sure you understand exactly what you're getting for every dollar, so you can make a confident decision."
When they push again:
> "I hear you, and I respect that you're being direct. Our pricing is set where it is because it's what allows us to deliver the service level that protects your asset. I'm not in a position to change that, but what I can do is make sure you feel completely confident in what you're getting. What specific part of the value are you still unsure about?"
When they threaten to go to the cheaper competitor:
> "That's completely your call, and I respect it. What I'd encourage you to ask them is: what does their fee not include? What happens when there's an eviction? What's their vacancy rate? What's their maintenance markup? Our clients have found that the total cost of ownership is lower with us, even at a higher management fee, because we prevent the expensive surprises. But you should make the decision that feels right to you."
#### Escalation Rule
---
Critical: Any fee deviation, no matter how small, requires manager approval before it is offered. The BDM never discounts unilaterally. If the owner is pushing hard and the BDM believes there is a legitimate business case (multi-door portfolio, strategic relationship), the BDM says: "Let me talk to my manager and see what we can do for a portfolio structure; I want to make sure I'm giving you the right answer." Then escalate within 24 hours. Manager decides. BDM communicates.
#### Red Lines: Never Do These
- Never offer a discount without manager approval
- Never match a competitor's price to close a deal
- Never agree to a verbal discount and document a different fee in the PMA
- Never promise a future fee reduction as a condition of signing
- Never discount one owner's fee without a documented, manager-approved business reason
### Scenario 2: The Owner with a Problem Property Nobody Should Take
#### What It Looks Like
The property has issues that go beyond normal make-ready. The BDM senses, or discovers, that the property is going to be a chronic problem: structurally compromised, in a location with no rental demand, priced at a rent that the market will never support, or in a condition that would require significant capital the owner refuses to spend.
Common versions:
- Property needs $15,000 in repairs before it can be shown; owner wants to list it now
- Property is in a flood zone, has unresolved code violations, or has a history of mold
- Owner insists on a rent rate 25%+ above market and refuses to adjust
- Property has features that will make it nearly unleasable (unusual layout, extreme customization, location with no comparable rentals)
- Owner has deferred maintenance for years and expects the PM to "figure it out"
#### Why It's Dangerous
- An unleasable property creates extended vacancy, which costs the owner money and costs you time with no revenue
- A property with unresolved code violations or habitability issues creates legal liability for the management company [LEGAL: in most states, the PM can be held liable for placing a tenant in a property with known habitability defects — consult your broker before taking on any property with unresolved violations]
- An owner who won't invest in the property won't approve necessary repairs after move-in, creating a maintenance nightmare and tenant dissatisfaction
- A property that sits vacant reflects on your leasing metrics and your reputation in the market
- Taking a property you know is wrong, just to hit a door count, is the most expensive short-term decision a BDM can make
#### The Judgment Call
| Situation | What to Do |
|---|---|
| Property needs work but owner is willing to invest and has a realistic timeline | Accept with conditions: document required make-ready items; do not begin marketing until complete |
| Property has unresolved code violations or habitability issues | Do not accept; escalate to manager; [LEGAL: do not place a tenant in a property with known violations] |
| Owner insists on rent 15 to 25%+ above market and refuses data | Do not accept at that price; offer to list at market rate or walk away |
| Property is in a location outside your service area or below your minimum rent threshold | Decline gracefully; refer to another manager |
| Property has cosmetic issues only and owner is motivated | Accept; standard make-ready process applies |
#### What to Say
Graceful bow-out for a property that doesn't meet standards:
> "We've been doing this for a long time, and some properties are more attractive to owners than they are to renters. Our renters are expecting certain features and conditions when they move into a new home, and I think we'd be doing you a disservice by marketing this property to our prospective tenants in its current state. I don't want to take your money and deliver a poor result; that's not who we are. Here's what I'd recommend instead…"
When the owner insists on an above-market rent:
> "I completely respect that you have a number in mind, and I want to help you get there. But the rent is set by the market, not by what we want, and not by what the mortgage requires. If we price above market, the property sits vacant, and every day it sits vacant costs you money. I'd rather get you a qualified tenant at market rate quickly than chase a number that the market won't support. Can we agree to list at market and adjust if we're wrong within the first two weeks?"
When the owner won't invest in required repairs:
> "I want to be straight with you because I think you deserve that. If we list this property in its current condition, we're going to attract the bottom of the applicant pool, and that creates bigger problems down the road. The investment you make now in getting this property rent-ready will come back to you in a better tenant, a faster lease, and fewer headaches. If that's not something you're able to do right now, I'd rather have an honest conversation about timing than set you up for a frustrating experience."
#### Escalation Rule
---
Critical: Any property with unresolved code violations, habitability concerns, structural issues, or a rent demand more than 20% above market must be escalated to the manager before the PMA is sent. The BDM does not make the call to accept or reject these properties alone. Manager reviews and decides. If the manager approves with conditions, those conditions are documented in writing before the PMA is executed.
#### Red Lines: Never Do These
- Never accept a property with known code violations or habitability defects without manager approval and a documented remediation plan
- Never list a property at a rent rate you know the market will not support just to get the signature
- Never promise an owner that a problem property will lease quickly
- Never take a property below your company's minimum rent threshold without manager approval
- Never accept a property outside your service area to hit a door count goal
### Scenario 3: The Owner Leaving Another Manager on Bad Terms
#### What It Looks Like
The owner is switching from a previous property management company. They are angry, frustrated, or feel wronged. They may be mid-lease, mid-dispute, or mid-eviction. They want you to take over immediately and fix everything the last manager did wrong.
Common versions:
- "My last PM was terrible; they never communicated, the tenant hasn't paid in three months, and I have no idea what's going on."
- "I'm suing my last property manager. Can you take over the property while the case is pending?"
- "The tenant has a lease that expires in two months but I want out of my current PM contract now."
- "My last manager lost my security deposit records; I don't have any documentation."
#### Why It's Dangerous
- The owner may be partly responsible for the problems they're describing, and you're only hearing one side
- Taking over a property mid-dispute, mid-eviction, or mid-litigation creates legal exposure for your company [LEGAL: consult your broker before accepting any property with pending litigation involving the previous PM or the current tenant]
- Missing documentation (no move-in inspection, no deposit ledger, no signed lease) creates immediate liability: if the tenant disputes the deposit at move-out, you have no baseline
- An owner who blames everything on the previous manager may be an owner who will blame everything on you next
- The emotional state of a burned owner creates unrealistic expectations for the new relationship
#### The Judgment Call
| Situation | What to Do |
|---|---|
| Owner is leaving previous PM due to poor communication; property is stable, tenant is paying, documentation is complete | Accept; standard onboarding with full document verification |
| Owner has no move-in inspection, no deposit ledger, or no signed lease from previous manager | Accept only with conditions: document what is missing; conduct fresh inspection immediately; note all gaps in writing before accepting liability |
| Property has a pending eviction in progress | Escalate to manager; do not accept without manager and [LEGAL: legal counsel] review |
| Owner is in active litigation with previous PM | Escalate to manager and legal; do not accept without full legal review |
| Owner is angry, blaming, and making demands about what you will do differently before you've even seen the property | Slow down: this is a red flag for a difficult owner relationship; qualify motivation and expectations carefully before proceeding |
#### What to Say
Acknowledging the frustration without validating the blame:
> "I'm sorry you've had that experience; that's not what property management should feel like. Before we talk about what we can do for you, I want to make sure I understand the full picture so I can set the right expectations. Can you walk me through where things stand right now: the tenant, the lease, the documentation you have?"
When documentation is missing:
> "Here's what I want to be upfront about: without a move-in inspection and a deposit ledger, we're starting without a baseline. That creates risk for you at move-out: if the tenant disputes the deposit, we won't have the documentation to defend it. What I'd recommend is that we conduct a fresh inspection immediately when we take over, document everything we see, and note in writing what was not provided by the previous manager. That protects you going forward. Does that work for you?"
When the owner is making it clear they'll blame you for everything:
> "I want to make sure we start this relationship with the right expectations. We're very good at what we do, and we'll work hard for you, but property management involves variables we can't always control: market conditions, tenant behavior, maintenance surprises. What I can promise is that we'll communicate proactively, act in your best interest, and never leave you wondering what's happening. What I can't promise is that everything will be perfect. Is that a foundation you can work with?"
#### Escalation Rule
---
Critical: Any takeover involving pending eviction, active litigation, missing deposit documentation, or an owner who has expressed intent to pursue legal action against the previous manager must be escalated to the manager and reviewed by legal counsel before the PMA is executed. The BDM does not accept these properties independently.
#### Red Lines: Never Do These
- Never accept a property mid-eviction without manager and legal approval
- Never accept liability for a security deposit you did not collect and cannot verify
- Never promise an owner that you will "fix" what the previous manager did wrong without knowing what actually happened
- Never badmouth the previous property management company, even if the owner invites it
- Never accept a property in active litigation without legal review
### Scenario 4: The Lead Who Wants to Negotiate Every Clause
#### What It Looks Like
The owner has reviewed the PMA and comes back with a list of changes. They want to modify the termination clause, the maintenance authorization threshold, the fee structure, the liability language, or the exclusivity provision. They may be an attorney, a sophisticated investor, or simply someone who reads everything carefully.
Common versions:
- "I want to lower the maintenance authorization threshold from $500 to $200."
- "I want a 30-day termination clause instead of 90 days."
- "I want to remove the exclusivity clause. I may want to self-manage some units."
- "I want to add a clause that requires my approval on every tenant before you sign the lease."
- "I want to cap your liability waiver."
#### Why It's Dangerous
- A modified PMA that hasn't been reviewed by your broker or legal counsel creates legal exposure for the company [LEGAL: any modification to the standard PMA must be reviewed and approved by the broker of record before execution — the BDM has no authority to agree to contract changes]
- A $200 maintenance threshold means the owner must approve every minor repair, creating delays that damage tenant relationships and property condition
- A 30-day termination clause with no penalty means the owner can leave the moment a difficult situation arises, leaving your team holding an active tenant relationship with no contract
- An owner who wants approval on every tenant is signaling a desire to control the screening process, which creates fair housing risk [LEGAL: owner involvement in individual tenant selection decisions creates significant fair housing liability — consult your broker]
- A clause-by-clause negotiator before signing is a clause-by-clause challenger after signing
#### The Judgment Call
| Situation | What to Do |
|---|---|
| Owner has minor questions about language and wants clarification | Explain the purpose of each clause: most questions dissolve when the owner understands why the clause exists |
| Owner wants a lower maintenance threshold | Explain the operational impact: offer the standard threshold with a commitment to proactive communication |
| Owner wants a shorter termination clause | Escalate to manager; do not agree to non-standard termination terms without approval |
| Owner wants to remove exclusivity or retain tenant approval rights | Escalate to manager and legal: these are material changes with legal implications |
| Owner wants to redline multiple clauses and is treating it as a negotiation | Escalate to manager: this owner may not be a fit for your standard agreement |
#### What to Say
When the owner wants to change the maintenance threshold:
> "I completely understand wanting to stay in control of your costs; that's smart ownership. Here's the challenge: if we need your approval on every repair under $500, we're going to have delays. Tenants expect fast responses, and delays on small repairs lead to bigger problems and unhappy tenants. What we do instead is send you a notification every time a work order is issued, so you always know what's happening; you just don't have to be the bottleneck on small items. Does that give you the visibility you're looking for?"
When the owner wants to change termination terms:
> "I hear you, and I want to be transparent: I'm not in a position to change the contract terms on my own; that's a conversation that has to go through our broker. What I can do is make sure you understand why that clause is there, and then if you still want to explore a modification, I'll bring it to the right person. The termination clause protects both of us: it ensures we have enough time to transition the tenant relationship properly if things change. Can I explain how it works in practice?"
When the owner is treating the PMA as a negotiation:
> "I want to be straight with you, because I think you'd want that. Our agreement is standardized for a reason: it's been reviewed by our legal team and our broker, and it's designed to protect both you and us. I can explain every clause and why it's there, and I'm happy to do that. But I'm not able to modify the agreement at the table; any change has to go through our broker. If after that conversation there are still terms you can't accept, I want to know that now so we can figure out if we're the right fit for each other."
#### Escalation Rule
---
Critical: Any request to modify PMA language (any clause, any term, any threshold) must be escalated to the manager and broker of record before any response is given to the owner. The BDM never agrees to contract modifications, even verbally. The BDM's only authorized response is: "I'll need to bring that to our broker; let me get you an answer within [FILL: your standard turnaround, e.g., 24–48 hours]."
#### Red Lines: Never Do These
- Never agree to a contract modification verbally or in writing without broker approval
- Never tell an owner "we can work around that" as a substitute for a formal modification
- Never allow an owner to retain individual tenant approval rights: this is a fair housing liability
- Never lower the maintenance authorization threshold below your company's minimum without manager and legal approval
- Never execute a modified PMA without the broker of record's signature and documented approval
### Scenario 5: Pricing Against a Cheaper Competitor
#### What It Looks Like
The owner has shopped around. They have a quote from a competitor at a lower fee, sometimes significantly lower. They want to know why they should pay more for you. This is the most common objection in the listing appointment and the one BDMs handle worst when they're not prepared.
Common versions:
- "XYZ Property Management quoted me 8%; you're at 10%. Why should I pay more?"
- "The other company doesn't charge a setup fee."
- "The other company does tenant placement for one month's rent; you charge more."
- "I found a company online that charges a flat $99/month. Explain why you're worth more."
#### Why It's Dangerous
- Competing on price is a race to the bottom: if you win on price, you attract the owner who will always prioritize price over service
- A BDM who discounts to match a competitor signals that the company's value proposition is not strong enough to stand on its own
- The owner who chose you because you were cheapest will leave you the moment someone cheaper appears
- Cheaper competitors are almost always cheaper because they are doing less (fewer inspections, slower maintenance response, weaker screening, no protection programs) and the owner will feel that gap when something goes wrong
- The real cost comparison is total cost of ownership over the life of the lease, not the management fee percentage
#### The Judgment Call
| Situation | What to Do |
|---|---|
| Owner is using competitor price as leverage to get a discount | Hold the price; reframe on value; see script below; do not discount |
| Owner genuinely doesn't understand what they're comparing | Educate: walk them through what the competitor's fee does and does not include |
| Owner has a legitimate relationship with the competitor and is leaning that way | Make your best value case once, clearly, then let them decide; do not chase |
| Owner is comparing a full-service PM to a tenant-placement-only service | Clarify the comparison: these are not the same product |
| Owner insists on matching the competitor's price | Escalate to manager; do not match unilaterally; see Scenario 1 |
#### What to Say
The core value reframe:
> "I can see why you're concerned that our fees are higher than some of our competition. But our clients have found that it actually costs less to work with us over the lifetime of the lease. The ultimate costs you need to manage are vacancy, property damage, evictions, and surprises. Managing those well is why we're the less expensive choice, even at a higher fee. If they're not charging for something, they're probably not doing it. And when something goes wrong, you'll feel that gap."
Breaking down what the cheaper fee doesn't include:
> "When you compare us to a company at 8%, here's what I'd encourage you to ask them: What's their vacancy rate? What's their average days to lease? Do they have a protection program if the tenant doesn't pay? What's their maintenance markup? Do they conduct move-in and move-out video inspections? Do they require tenant liability insurance? We invest in professional photos, market on 90+ websites, have a dedicated leasing line answered 24/7, and carry protection programs that cover you for eviction costs and damage up to $2,500. That's what our fee pays for. If they're charging less, ask what's missing."
When the owner still pushes:
> "I respect that you're being thorough; that's exactly the kind of owner we want to work with. Here's my honest answer: I'm not going to match their price, because matching their price would mean delivering their service level. And that's not what we do. What I can do is make sure you feel completely confident that what you're getting from us is worth every dollar. What specific part of the value are you still unsure about?"
#### Escalation Rule
---
Critical: If an owner will not move forward without a price match and the BDM believes there is a legitimate business case, escalate to manager before making any pricing concession. The BDM's position at the table is always: "I'm not able to change our pricing, but let me make sure you understand exactly what you're getting." Any deviation from standard pricing requires manager approval. See Scenario 1 escalation rule.
#### Red Lines: Never Do These
- Never match a competitor's price to close a deal
- Never disparage a competitor by name: reframe on your value, not their failures
- Never imply the competitor is dishonest; only note what their fee may or may not include
- Never tell an owner the competitor is "terrible" even if you know they are
- Never let a competitor's price become the anchor for your negotiation
### Scenario 6: When to Walk Away from a Deal
#### What It Looks Like
Not every owner who wants to sign should sign. The BDM's job is not to close every deal; it is to close the right deals. Walking away is a skill, and it requires the same judgment and confidence as closing.
Common signals that a deal should not be closed:
| Signal | What It Predicts |
|---|---|
| Owner has negotiated the fee, the contract terms, and the maintenance threshold, and is still pushing | Chronic boundary-pusher; will challenge every decision post-signing |
| Owner insists on approving every tenant personally | Fair housing liability; will slow every placement; will blame you when they don't like the applicant |
| Owner's rent expectation is 20%+ above market and they refuse data | Property will sit vacant; owner will blame the PM; relationship will deteriorate |
| Owner is leaving their third PM in three years | The common denominator is the owner, not the PMs |
| Owner has expressed that they want to be "very involved" in day-to-day decisions | This owner does not actually want a property manager; they want an assistant |
| Property has unresolved legal, structural, or habitability issues owner refuses to address | Liability exposure; unleasable property; no-win situation |
| Owner has made statements that suggest discriminatory preferences [LEGAL: any owner who expresses preferences based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, disability, or any other protected class must be declined — this is a non-negotiable legal requirement] | Fair housing violation risk; do not accept |
| Owner is in financial distress and the rental income is their only way to cover a delinquent mortgage | High-pressure situation; any vacancy or late payment creates a crisis; owner will be impossible to manage |
| Owner has been hostile, dismissive, or disrespectful during the sales process | This behavior will be worse after signing, not better |
#### The Judgment Call
Walking away is not failure. It is the highest-value decision a BDM can make when the signals are clear. The cost of a bad owner relationship (in staff time, emotional energy, legal exposure, and reputation) far exceeds the revenue from one door.
The three-question test before walking away:
1. Can we serve this owner at our standard? If no, walk away.
2. Will this owner allow us to do our job? If no, walk away.
3. Does the risk of this property or this owner outweigh the revenue? If yes, walk away.
#### What to Say
The graceful decline for a property that doesn't fit:
> "It looks like your property doesn't fit our model based on [location / condition / pricing requirements]. Our portfolio is built around a specific type of property and owner experience, and I want to be honest with you rather than take your business and deliver a result that doesn't meet your expectations. I do know some other managers who might be a better fit; would you like me to share their names?"
The graceful decline for an owner who wants too much control:
> "I want to be straight with you, because I think you'd want that. The way you're describing your involvement in the day-to-day (approving tenants, approving repairs, being contacted before decisions are made) that's not really property management. That's co-management. And in my experience, that model doesn't work well for either party. What you need is a manager you can trust completely to act in your best interest without needing your approval at every step. If you're not there yet, that's okay, but I don't think we're the right fit right now."
The graceful decline for a discriminatory owner:
> "I have to be direct with you: what you're describing isn't something we're able to do. Fair housing law requires that we evaluate every applicant on the same objective criteria (income, credit, rental history) regardless of [protected characteristic]. We can't restrict who we market to or who we screen based on that. It's not a policy we can bend; it's the law, and it protects you as much as it protects the applicants. If that's a requirement for working with us, then I don't think we're the right fit."
#### Escalation Rule
---
Critical: Any decision to walk away from a deal, especially one involving a fair housing concern, a property with legal issues, or an owner with multiple red flags, must be documented in the CRM with the specific reason and escalated to the manager before the final conversation with the owner. The BDM does not walk away from a deal without the manager knowing why. This protects the BDM, the company, and creates a record if the owner later makes a complaint.
#### Red Lines: Never Do These
- Never accept a deal you know is wrong just to hit a door count goal
- Never accept an owner who has expressed discriminatory preferences: this is a legal non-negotiable
- Never walk away from a deal without documenting the reason in the CRM
- Never tell an owner they are being declined because of their personality; use the property or fit language above
- Never let a manager pressure you to close a deal you have flagged as a legal or ethical risk
### Scenario 7: The Handoff Where Operations Finds Surprises the BDM Missed
#### What It Looks Like
The PMA is signed. The BDM hands the deal to the operations or onboarding team. During the intake process, the ops team discovers information that was not captured during the sales process, and that changes the picture significantly.
Common surprises:
- Property has an HOA with rental restrictions the BDM didn't ask about
- Owner has a tenant in place but has no signed lease, no deposit record, and no move-in inspection
- Property has deferred maintenance that wasn't disclosed: HVAC hasn't been serviced in four years, roof is near end of life
- Owner's mortgage is delinquent; discovered during document collection
- Owner entity on the PMA doesn't match the actual ownership entity on the tax record
- Owner has a pending insurance claim on the property that wasn't mentioned
- Property has a history of code violations the owner didn't disclose
#### Why It's Dangerous
- Surprises at handoff damage the relationship between sales and operations, and erode trust in the BDM's thoroughness
- Some surprises create legal liability that the company has now inherited by signing the PMA
- An owner who withheld information during the sales process will continue to withhold information during the management relationship
- Repeated handoff surprises are a training and accountability issue: they signal that the BDM's discovery process is incomplete
#### The Judgment Call
| Surprise | What to Do |
|---|---|
| HOA rental restrictions discovered post-signing | Escalate immediately to manager; [LEGAL: review whether the PMA can be executed given the restriction; consult broker] |
| Missing tenant documentation (no lease, no deposit record, no inspection) | Do not proceed with tenant onboarding until documentation is resolved; notify owner in writing of the gap and the risk |
| Deferred maintenance discovered at inspection | Notify owner immediately with written documentation; require repair plan before marketing begins |
| Delinquent mortgage discovered | Escalate to manager immediately; [LEGAL: consult broker — managing a property in foreclosure risk creates significant liability] |
| Ownership entity mismatch | Do not proceed until PMA is re-executed with the correct legal entity; [LEGAL: a PMA signed by the wrong party may be unenforceable] |
| Undisclosed insurance claim | Escalate to manager; document in writing; assess impact on property condition and coverage |
| History of code violations | Escalate to manager and legal; do not place a tenant until violations are resolved |
#### What the BDM Must Do When Ops Finds a Surprise
1. Own it: do not blame the owner for not disclosing; the BDM's job is to ask the right questions
2. Document it: log the surprise in the CRM with a full description and the date it was discovered
3. Escalate it: notify the manager immediately; do not let ops handle it alone
4. Fix the process: after resolution, identify which discovery call question would have caught this and add it to the intake checklist
5. Communicate with the owner: the BDM, not ops, should have the conversation with the owner about what was found and what needs to happen next
#### What to Say to the Owner
When a significant surprise is discovered:
> "I want to be upfront with you about something we found during our onboarding process. [Describe the issue clearly and factually.] This is something we need to address before we can move forward, because it affects [the property's leasability / your legal protection / our ability to manage effectively]. Here's what I'd recommend we do…"
When the owner withheld information:
> "I want to make sure we're starting this relationship with full transparency; that's the only way we can do our job well. Going forward, if there's anything about the property or the tenant situation that changes, I need to know immediately. The more we know, the better we can protect you."
#### The BDM's Prevention Checklist
Ask every one of these questions before the PMA is signed:
| Question | What It Catches |
|---|---|
| Is there an HOA? If yes, are there any rental restrictions? | HOA rental caps or approval requirements |
| Is the property currently occupied? If yes, do you have the signed lease, deposit ledger, and move-in inspection? | Missing tenant documentation |
| When was the HVAC last serviced? Roof age? Any known deferred maintenance? | Undisclosed maintenance issues |
| Is the mortgage current? | Foreclosure risk |
| What is the exact legal name of the ownership entity? Can you provide the entity documentation? | Ownership entity mismatch |
| Are there any open insurance claims on the property? | Undisclosed claims |
| Have there been any code violations or city notices in the last 24 months? | Unresolved violations |
| Are there any pending legal matters involving the property or the current tenant? | Litigation risk |
#### Escalation Rule
---
Critical: Any surprise discovered at handoff that involves legal exposure, ownership entity issues, missing tenant documentation, delinquent mortgage, or unresolved code violations must be escalated to the manager within 24 hours of discovery. Onboarding does not proceed on any of these items until the manager has reviewed and approved a path forward. The BDM is accountable for the quality of the information delivered at handoff; repeated surprises are a performance issue.
#### Red Lines: Never Do These
- Never allow onboarding to proceed on a property with unresolved code violations or habitability issues
- Never execute a PMA with an ownership entity that doesn't match the tax record
- Never accept a tenant-occupied property without the signed lease, deposit ledger, and move-in inspection, or a documented plan to address what's missing
- Never let ops absorb a handoff surprise without the BDM being accountable for the gap
- Never proceed with tenant placement on a property with a delinquent mortgage without manager and legal review
### Scenario 8: Promising Service Levels Sales Should Never Promise
#### What It Looks Like
In the excitement of closing a deal, a BDM makes a commitment that operations cannot keep, or that creates a legal or financial liability the company never agreed to. These promises feel harmless in the moment. They become the source of the most damaging owner complaints, the most difficult ops conversations, and the most expensive disputes.
Common versions:
- "We'll have a tenant in 30 days, guaranteed."
- "We'll get you $2,200/month; I'm confident."
- "We'll call you every week with an update."
- "We'll approve your repair vendors if you want to use your own guys."
- "We'll waive the leasing fee if it takes longer than 60 days."
- "We'll make sure the tenant takes care of the lawn; we'll check every month."
- "You'll never have to worry about anything; we handle everything."
#### Why It's Dangerous
- A promise made by sales becomes an expectation held by the owner, and a complaint when ops doesn't deliver it
- Specific rent guarantees create financial liability if the market doesn't support the number
- Specific timeline guarantees create liability if the property doesn't lease in the promised window
- Promising custom communication cadences that ops can't sustain creates a service gap from day one
- Allowing owner-selected vendors bypasses your vendor vetting process and creates quality and liability risk
- "We handle everything" is the most dangerous promise of all, because it sets an expectation of perfection that no PM company can deliver
### The Never-Promise List
This is the complete list of commitments a BDM is never authorized to make, under any circumstances, to any owner, at any stage of the sales process.
#### Never Promise: Leasing and Vacancy
| Never Promise | Why | What to Say Instead |
|---|---|---|
| A specific number of days to place a tenant | Market conditions, property condition, and pricing all affect leasing time; you cannot guarantee a timeline you don't control | "Our marketing system is designed to lease properties as quickly as possible. In [your market], well-priced, rent-ready properties typically lease in [X] days. We'll know within the first two weeks if we're priced correctly." |
| A specific tenant placement date | Same as above; too many variables outside your control | "Once the property is rent-ready and listed, we move fast. I can't give you a date, but I can tell you we don't let properties sit." |
| That the property will lease in under 28 days unless your company has a documented, contractual guarantee program for that specific market | A guarantee you can't back up creates a financial liability | "We have a leasing guarantee program; let me explain exactly how it works and what it covers." [FILL: only reference your guarantee if it is a documented, approved program in your market] |
| That you will waive or reduce the leasing fee if the property doesn't lease within a certain timeframe | This is a fee modification; requires manager approval | "I'm not able to make that commitment, but let me tell you what we do to make sure we're moving as fast as possible." |
#### Never Promise: Rent Rate and Financial Performance
| Never Promise | Why | What to Say Instead |
|---|---|---|
| A specific rent amount | The market sets the rent, not the PM | "Based on our market analysis, the range is $X to $X. We'll price it to maximize your return and adjust quickly if the market tells us something different." |
| That the owner will net a specific monthly income | Too many variables: vacancy, maintenance, fees, market shifts | "Here's how to think about your expected net: let me walk you through the math based on market rent, our fees, and typical maintenance costs. These are estimates, not guarantees." |
| That the property will cash-flow positively | If the mortgage is high relative to market rent, it may not | "Let's run the numbers together so you have a realistic picture before we move forward." |
| That rent will increase at renewal | Market conditions determine renewal rates | "We'll conduct a market analysis at renewal and recommend the appropriate rate at that time." |
#### Never Promise: Maintenance and Property Condition
| Never Promise | Why | What to Say Instead |
|---|---|---|
| That the tenant will take care of the property | Tenant behavior cannot be guaranteed; only screening quality and inspection frequency | "We screen carefully and inspect regularly. We can't guarantee tenant behavior, but we can catch problems early and hold tenants accountable." |
| That maintenance costs will stay below a specific number | Maintenance is unpredictable: HVAC fails, roofs leak, appliances break | "We work hard to keep costs down: we troubleshoot before dispatching, use vetted vendors, and require your approval on anything above $[FILL: your threshold]. But I can't put a cap on what the property will need." |
| That the owner's preferred vendors will be used | Your vendor vetting process exists for quality and liability reasons | "We have a vetted vendor network that we trust. If you have a vendor you've used before, we can evaluate them, but I can't promise we'll use them until they've been through our process." |
| That a specific repair will be completed by a specific date | Vendor availability, parts, and property access all affect timelines | "We'll move as quickly as possible. I can't give you a date until we have the vendor scheduled, but we'll keep you updated every step of the way." |
| That the property will be returned in the same condition it was rented | Normal wear and tear is the owner's responsibility by law. [LEGAL: most states prohibit charging tenants for normal wear and tear; consult your broker] | "We document condition thoroughly at move-in and move-out. We'll hold tenants accountable for damage beyond normal wear and tear, but some wear is expected and is not recoverable." |
| That inspections will catch every problem | Inspections are visual and periodic; they are not a guarantee of property condition | "We conduct inspections at [FILL: your schedule — e.g., move-in, 60 days, 10 months, move-out]. These give us a strong baseline and catch most issues early, but they are not a guarantee that nothing will be missed." |
#### Never Promise: Communication and Owner Experience
| Never Promise | Why | What to Say Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly calls or a custom communication cadence not in your standard service model | Ops cannot sustain a custom cadence for every owner, and when it lapses, it becomes a complaint | "Here's our standard communication process: [describe your actual model]. You'll also have 24/7 access to your owner portal where you can see everything in real time." |
| That the owner will always be able to reach someone immediately | Response times depend on staffing, hours, and urgency level | "We have a dedicated line for maintenance emergencies that is answered 24/7. For non-emergency questions, our team responds within [FILL: your standard SLA, e.g., one business day]." |
| That the owner will never be surprised by a cost | Maintenance emergencies, legal costs, and market shifts create unexpected expenses | "We'll never spend above $[FILL: your threshold] without your approval on non-emergency items. But I want to be honest: surprises happen in property management. Our job is to minimize them and communicate fast when they occur." |
| That the owner will be consulted on every decision | This is co-management, not property management, and it creates fair housing risk on tenant decisions | "You'll be consulted on anything above your maintenance threshold and on any major lease decisions. Day-to-day management is handled by our team; that's what you're paying us for." |
| That you will "handle everything" with no owner involvement ever | This sets an expectation of perfection and zero friction, which no PM company can deliver | "We handle the heavy lifting: marketing, screening, maintenance coordination, rent collection, compliance. You'll still need to be available for major decisions and approvals. But we make it as hands-off as possible." |
#### Never Promise: Legal and Compliance
| Never Promise | Why | What to Say Instead |
|---|---|---|
| That an eviction will be resolved in a specific timeframe | Court timelines are outside your control. [LEGAL: eviction timelines vary significantly by state, county, and judge; never quote a specific timeline] | "We file quickly and follow the legal process precisely. Timelines depend on the court: in [your market], the typical process takes [FILL: your market's general range], but I can't guarantee a specific date." |
| That the owner will win an eviction or security deposit dispute | Legal outcomes are never guaranteed | "We document everything to give you the strongest possible position. But legal outcomes are up to the court; I can't promise a result." |
| That your screening process will prevent all bad tenants | No screening process is perfect. [LEGAL: screening criteria must be applied consistently to all applicants to avoid fair housing violations] | "Our 14-point screening process eliminates the vast majority of high-risk applicants. But no screening process can guarantee tenant behavior; that's why we have our protection programs." |
| That the owner can reject a specific applicant for a reason not in your screening criteria | This is a fair housing violation risk | "Applicant decisions are made on our objective screening criteria: income, credit, rental history, criminal background. I can't promise that a specific person will or won't be approved." |
| That your protection programs cover all possible losses | Protection programs have defined limits and conditions. [LEGAL: never overstate coverage; owners should read the program terms] | "Our protection program covers [FILL: your specific program terms — e.g., up to $2,500 in damage beyond the deposit, eviction filing fees, and rent protection]. Here's exactly what it includes and what it doesn't." |
#### Never Promise: Fees and Contract Terms
| Never Promise | Why | What to Say Instead |
|---|---|---|
| That fees will never increase | Fee structures may change with market conditions or service additions | "Our current fee structure is [X]. We'll always give you advance notice of any changes; you'll never be surprised." |
| That the owner can cancel at any time with no consequences | The PMA has a termination clause for a reason; consult your broker on your specific terms | "Our agreement has a [FILL: your termination notice period] notice requirement. We also offer a 90-day satisfaction guarantee: if we're not living up to our commitments, you can exit without penalty in that window." |
| That a referral fee will be paid in a specific amount or timeframe without a signed referral agreement | Verbal referral fee commitments create disputes | "Referral fees are documented in a signed agreement before they're paid. Let me get you the details in writing." |
| That the management fee covers services not in your standard agreement | Scope creep starts with a verbal promise at the listing appointment | "Everything we do is outlined in the agreement. If you need something outside that scope, we can discuss it, but I want to make sure we're both clear on what's included before we sign." |
### The Master Escalation Rule
---
The BDM seat has authority to present, explain, and close, not to modify, guarantee, or commit beyond the standard agreement.
Every scenario in this guide resolves to the same escalation framework.
#### Escalation Decision Tree
Owner asks for something outside standard:
| Question | Action |
|---|---|
| Is it a fee modification? | Escalate to manager. Never agree unilaterally. |
| Is it a contract modification? | Escalate to manager + broker. Never agree unilaterally. |
| Is it a service level promise? | Check the Never-Promise List. If it's on the list, do not promise it. |
| Is it a legal or compliance question? | Escalate to manager + legal. Never give a legal opinion. |
| Is it a property acceptance call? | Escalate to manager for any red-flag property. |
| Is it a walk-away decision? | Escalate to manager before the final conversation with the owner. |
#### Escalation Response Scripts
When you need to escalate before answering:
> "That's a great question and I want to give you the right answer, not just a fast one. Let me bring that to my manager and get back to you within [FILL: e.g., 24 hours]. I'd rather take a day and give you the accurate answer than commit to something I'm not authorized to commit to."
When you need to escalate a contract modification:
> "Any change to our standard agreement has to go through our broker; that's not something I can approve at the table. I'll bring it to them today and get you an answer by [specific date/time]. Does that work?"
When you need to escalate a walk-away:
> "I want to make sure I'm giving you the right answer here; let me talk to my manager today and come back to you. I don't want to make a decision this important without making sure we've thought it through on our end."
#### Escalation Log: What to Document Every Time
Every escalation must be logged in the CRM before the manager conversation, not after. The log entry must include:
| Field | What to Write |
|---|---|
| Date and time | When the situation arose |
| What the owner asked for or said | Exact words if possible |
| Why it requires escalation | Which scenario, which rule |
| BDM's recommended path | What the BDM thinks should happen |
| Manager decision | What was decided and by whom |
| What was communicated to the owner | Exact response given |
| Outcome | Deal won / lost / modified / walked away |
