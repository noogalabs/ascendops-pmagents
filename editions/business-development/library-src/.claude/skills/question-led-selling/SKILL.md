# The Method

**People are persuaded by what they conclude, not by what they are told.**

Everything below follows from that one line. The job is not to explain the company well. It is to ask the questions that let an owner arrive somewhere on their own — because a conclusion they reached survives the drive home, and a pitch they heard does not.

This is question-led, conclusion-driven selling: the prospect is persuaded by what they conclude, not what they are told. The framing is the seat's craft; the specifics of what the company offers come from `business-development-config.json`.

---

## Three Rules

**1. Lower resistance, always.**
Pressure creates resistance. The harder the push, the harder the pull back. Stay neutral, calm, curious. A slightly detached tone — *"I'm honestly not sure this is even a fit for you"* — lowers a guard that enthusiasm raises. Sounding like you need the deal is the fastest way to not get it.

**2. Ask, do not tell.**
Telling triggers skepticism. Asking triggers reflection. If you are explaining, you are losing ground you will have to win back.

**3. Let them feel the gap.**
Nobody changes toward a solution. They change to escape a problem they have fully felt. Situation → gap → consequence, and only then the offer. Presenting before the gap is felt turns the best part of the offer into noise.

## The Stage Flow

### Connection
Lower the guard before asking for anything. On outbound, a pattern interrupt beats an introduction — an introduction tells them what box to put you in. Ask permission before asking questions.

> "Hey — this is [name], I'm probably calling out of the blue here. Do you have thirty seconds for me to tell you why I'm calling, and you can tell me if it's worth continuing?"

### Engagement — the heart of it

Four question types, in order. Do not skip forward; the later ones do not work without the earlier ones.

**Situation** — neutral, factual, easy to answer.
> "How are you handling tenants, turns, and maintenance across your rentals right now?"
> "How long have you been renting this one out?"

**Problem awareness** — where the friction is, in their words.
> "What's the part of it that takes the most out of you?"
> "When something breaks at nine at night, what happens?"

**Solution awareness** — what they have already tried, and what they actually want.
> "Have you looked at bringing in a manager before? What made you not go ahead?"
> "If this were running the way you wanted it to, what would be different?"

**Consequence** — the question most people skip, and the one that does the work.
> "If nothing changes in the next twelve months, what does that look like for you?"
> "You mentioned it's eating your weekends. What's that actually costing you?"

Then be quiet. The pause after a consequence question is where the deal is made. Filling it with reassurance undoes the whole sequence.

### Transition
Ask for permission to move, rather than announcing that you are moving.
> "That makes a lot of sense. Would it help if I showed you how we'd handle that specific piece?"

### Presentation
Now — and not before — the offer, mapped to what they said. Only the parts that answer what they told you. See `pricing-presentation` for rate and fee structure, and read `never-promise-list` before saying any number, date, or outcome out loud.

### Commitment
Book the next step while the awareness is at its peak. Two named options, never an open question.
> "Would [day] or [day] work better?"

## Tone

Do:
- Mirror their own words back as questions
- Let pauses sit
- Slow the tempo — a calm pace reads as confidence
- Be comfortable with "no"; chasing it is what makes it permanent

Do not:
- Pitch before the gap is felt
- Sound excited, needy, or eager
- Argue with an objection — see `objection-handling`
- Use false scarcity or manufactured urgency
- Talk more than the owner

---

## Empathy Is The Engine, Not A Tactic

These lower resistance because they are true, and they stop working the moment they are performed:

- "I get it — that sounds genuinely frustrating. What have you tried so far?"
- "No pressure either way. I'm honestly just trying to work out whether this is worth your time."
- "That's fair. A lot of owners feel the same way at first."

Empathy never tips into flattery, pressure, or agreeing with something that is not true. Agreeing that the previous manager was terrible is not empathy — it is a claim about a company you know nothing about, and it tells the owner exactly how you will talk about them one day.

## Audience Variations

| Audience | Open with |
|---|---|
| Cold outbound | Pattern interrupt + permission + a situation question |
| Inbound | Speed, then "what made you reach out?" — then situation and problem awareness before anything about the offer |
| Referral | Name the referrer, then straight to situation. The referral already did the connection work |
| Re-engaged nurture | What changed since last time. Never "just checking in" |

## Message Style

Short. Curious. One idea per message. A question beats a statement almost every time.

**Never write:**
- "I'd love to hop on a quick call to show you what we do!" — pitch-first
- "Limited-time offer" — false scarcity
- "We're the number one company in the area" — claim-first, triggers exactly the skepticism you are trying to avoid
- "Just following up!" — a bare nudge, and they are banned

**Write instead:**
- "Quick question — how are you handling maintenance across the two properties right now?"
- "Out of curiosity, what made you reach out?"
- "Makes sense. When you say it's been a headache, what's it actually costing you?"
- "No pressure either way — worth fifteen minutes, or is now just not the time?"
