# Maintenance Coordinator Judgment Guide (Generic Baseline)

## Maintenance Coordinator Judgment Guide (Generic Baseline)
Source: BetterWho session 2026-08-21 (AI-generated, verify with your broker and counsel). Generic baseline; companion to the Occupied Unit Maintenance Workflow and Board.
The Twelve Hard Scenarios: What Happened, What You Do Right Now, What You Never Do, When It Goes to the PM, and What Gets Written Down
---
How to use this guide: Every scenario follows the same five-part format. Read all twelve before your first solo week. Return to it whenever a situation feels off. The junior coordinator who guesses wrong on any of these twelve scenarios creates legal exposure, financial loss, or a broken owner/resident relationship, sometimes all three at once.
---
THE GOVERNING RULE FOR THE COORDINATOR SEAT
The coordinator's job is to move work forward and document everything, not to make judgment calls that belong to the PM.
Speed matters. Documentation matters more. When you are unsure whether a decision is yours to make, it is not. Escalate, document, and keep the resident informed. Those three actions, done in order, will protect you, the PM, the owner, and the company in every scenario below.
### Scenario 1: A Resident Emergency Claim That May Not Be One
#### What Happened
A resident calls or submits a work order marked "EMERGENCY." When you read it or ask follow-up questions, it is not clear whether this is a genuine life-safety emergency or an urgent-but-not-emergency situation the resident has escalated out of frustration or anxiety.
Common versions:
- "My heat is making a noise. I think it's going to explode."
- "There's water on my bathroom floor." (Could be a burst pipe. Could be a dripping faucet.)
- "I smell something weird. I think it might be gas."
- "My lock isn't working right. I don't feel safe."
- "There's a crack in my ceiling." (Could be active structural failure. Could be cosmetic settling.)
#### What You Do Right Now
Step 1: Call the resident immediately. Do not rely on the written description alone. Ask:
- "Can you describe exactly what you're seeing/smelling/hearing right now?"
- "Is anyone in the unit in immediate danger?"
- "When did this start and has it gotten worse?"
- "Can you send me a photo or video right now?"
Step 2: Triage using the emergency test. If the answer to ANY of these is YES → treat as emergency:
- Is there a risk of fire, explosion, or gas leak?
- Is there active flooding or water intrusion that is spreading?
- Is there a structural failure in progress?
- Is there a risk of injury if the resident stays in the unit?
- Is there no heat in freezing temperatures or no water at all?
- Is there a security breach: broken lock, broken window, forced entry?
Step 3: If YES to any emergency test question:
- Dispatch your emergency vendor immediately; do not wait for owner approval on life-safety items
- Notify the PM within 15 minutes
- Tell the resident: "We are dispatching someone now. Please [stay out of the area / call 911 if you smell gas / leave the unit if you feel unsafe]."
Step 4: If NO to all emergency test questions:
- Reclassify the work order to Urgent or Standard
- Communicate the reclassification to the resident with a clear timeline
- Log the reclassification decision and your reason in the work order notes
#### What You Never Do
- Never dismiss a resident's emergency claim without calling them first
- Never reclassify an emergency to standard without documenting your reasoning
- Never tell a resident their concern "isn't really an emergency" in those words; reframe on the timeline you're providing
- Never wait for owner approval before dispatching on a life-safety emergency
- Never assume a gas smell is not a gas leak; if the resident reports any gas odor, treat it as an emergency and tell them to leave the unit and call the gas company immediately
#### When It Goes to the PM
---
Critical: Immediately. Any call where you dispatched an emergency vendor, any situation involving potential gas/fire/flood/structural risk, any situation where the resident expressed fear for their safety, and any situation where you are genuinely unsure whether it is an emergency.
#### What Gets Written Down
- Time and method of resident contact
- Exact description of the issue as reported by the resident
- Your triage questions and the resident's answers
- Your classification decision and the reason
- If dispatched: vendor name, time dispatched, ETA given to resident
- If reclassified: new category, timeline communicated to resident, resident's response
### Scenario 2: No Heat or No Water in Extreme Weather
#### What Happened
A resident reports no heat during a freeze warning or no running water during extreme cold. This is not a judgment call about whether it is an emergency. It is. The judgment calls are about what you do in the next 60 minutes and what you do if the owner will not approve the repair.
#### What You Do Right Now
Immediate, within 15 minutes of the report:
- Confirm the outage is real: ask the resident to check the thermostat, breaker, and pilot light (for heat) or confirm whether neighbors are also affected (for water)
- Dispatch your emergency HVAC or plumbing vendor immediately; do not wait for owner approval
- Notify the PM immediately; this is a habitability issue with legal implications in virtually every state [LEGAL: most states require landlords to provide heat and running water as a condition of habitability; failure to restore within a legally defined timeframe can give the resident the right to withhold rent, repair-and-deduct, or terminate the lease — consult your broker for your state's specific requirements]
- Notify the owner: not for approval, but to inform them of the situation and the action being taken
- Give the resident a realistic ETA and check in every hour until resolved
If the repair cannot be completed same-day:
- Escalate to PM immediately
- PM determines whether temporary accommodations, space heaters, or rent abatement are appropriate
- Document every communication with the resident
#### What You Never Do
- Never wait for owner approval before dispatching on a no-heat or no-water emergency in extreme weather
- Never tell a resident to "just use extra blankets" or "buy bottled water" as a resolution; these are temporary measures only, not solutions
- Never go more than one hour without updating the resident on status
- Never close the work order until heat or water is fully restored and confirmed by the resident
- Never let an owner's refusal to approve the repair stop you from escalating to the PM; the PM makes the call on habitability, not the coordinator
#### When It Goes to the PM
---
Critical: Immediately upon confirmation of the outage. No-heat and no-water in extreme weather are never coordinator-level decisions to manage alone. The PM must be in the loop from the first dispatch.
#### What Gets Written Down
- Time of report and how it was received
- Confirmation steps taken and resident's responses
- Time vendor was dispatched and vendor name
- Time owner was notified and their response (or non-response)
- Time PM was notified
- Every resident update: time, method, content
- Time heat/water was restored and resident confirmation
### Scenario 3: A Vendor Asking for More Money Mid-Job
#### What Happened
A vendor is on-site doing an approved repair. They call or text to say the job is going to cost more than the original quote, sometimes significantly more. They may be asking for verbal approval to continue. The coordinator feels pressure to say yes so the job gets done.
Common versions:
- "I got in there and the pipe is worse than I thought. It's going to be $400 more."
- "The part I quoted isn't available. The replacement is more expensive."
- "There's mold behind the wall. I need to do remediation before I can finish."
- "The previous repair was done wrong. I have to redo it before I can fix this."
#### What You Do Right Now
Step 1: Do not give verbal approval. Tell the vendor:
> "I hear you. I need to get approval before you go further. Can you give me 15 minutes? Please don't proceed past what was originally quoted until I get back to you."
Step 2: Get the details in writing. Ask the vendor to text or email:
- The original quoted scope and price
- What they found that changed the scope
- The new total cost
- Photos of what they found
Step 3: Check the owner's maintenance authorization threshold.
- If the new total is within the owner's pre-approved threshold: you may be able to approve; check your company's specific authorization rules and confirm with PM
- If the new total exceeds the owner's threshold: escalate to PM immediately; do not approve
Step 4: Notify the PM with the vendor's written explanation and photos.
Step 5: PM contacts the owner for approval if required.
Step 6: Communicate the decision to the vendor with a clear timeline.
#### What You Never Do
- Never give verbal approval for a cost increase without written documentation from the vendor
- Never approve a cost that exceeds the owner's authorization threshold without PM and owner approval
- Never tell the vendor to "just finish it and we'll sort out the cost later"
- Never pay an invoice that reflects a cost you did not approve in advance
- Never let the vendor's time pressure push you into an unauthorized approval
#### When It Goes to the PM
---
Critical: Any cost increase that would push the total above the owner's authorization threshold goes to the PM before the vendor is told to proceed. If the increase is within threshold but involves a new scope item (e.g., mold, structural damage, code compliance), escalate to PM regardless of dollar amount.
#### What Gets Written Down
- Original quote amount and scope
- Vendor's explanation of the change: in their words, in writing
- Photos received from vendor
- New cost requested
- Whether the new total exceeds the owner's threshold
- PM notification: time and response
- Owner notification: time and response
- Final approval given: by whom, at what amount, at what time
- Instruction given to vendor and time given
### Scenario 4: A Tech Reporting the Problem Is Bigger Than Quoted
#### What Happened
This is the on-site version of Scenario 3, but the discovery is more significant. The vendor or technician finds something that changes the entire nature of the job: a structural issue, a code violation, a safety hazard, or a system failure that requires a specialist the original vendor cannot handle.
Common versions:
- HVAC tech finds the unit needs full replacement, not a repair
- Plumber finds the main line is collapsed, not just clogged
- Electrician finds the panel is unsafe and won't pass inspection
- Roofer finds decking damage that requires a full section replacement
- Contractor opens a wall and finds asbestos or mold
#### What You Do Right Now
Step 1: Stop the job. Tell the vendor:
> "Please stop where you are and don't proceed further. I need to escalate this before we go any further."
Step 2: Get documentation immediately:
- Photos and video of everything the vendor found
- Written description of what was found and what is needed
- Vendor's written estimate for the expanded scope
- If a safety hazard: ask the vendor whether the unit is safe for the resident to remain in
Step 3: Escalate to PM immediately. This is not a coordinator-level decision under any circumstances.
Step 4: If the vendor has identified a safety hazard:
- PM determines whether the resident needs to be temporarily relocated
- [LEGAL: if the unit is uninhabitable due to a discovered hazard, the PM must be involved in all decisions about resident communication, relocation, and rent abatement — do not make these commitments as a coordinator]
Step 5: Do not communicate the full scope of the problem to the resident until the PM has been briefed and has approved the messaging.
#### What You Never Do
- Never authorize a full system replacement without PM and owner approval
- Never tell the resident the full extent of the problem before the PM has been briefed
- Never let the vendor continue working on an expanded scope without written approval
- Never dismiss a vendor's safety concern; if they say it's unsafe, treat it as unsafe until proven otherwise
- Never close the original work order and open a new one to hide the cost increase; document everything in the original work order
#### When It Goes to the PM
---
Critical: Immediately. The moment the vendor reports a scope change that involves safety, structural integrity, code compliance, full system replacement, or hazardous materials. This is never a coordinator-level call.
#### What Gets Written Down
- Original work order scope and quoted cost
- Time vendor reported the expanded scope
- Exact description of what was found: vendor's words
- Photos and video received
- Whether a safety hazard was identified and vendor's exact statement
- Time PM was notified and PM's response
- Whether the job was stopped and at what point
- Owner notification: time and response
- Approved next steps and who approved them
### Scenario 5: An Owner Refusing a Habitability Repair
#### What Happened
A repair is required to maintain the property in a habitable condition: working heat, hot water, functioning plumbing, no active leaks, no pest infestation, no mold. The owner has been notified and is refusing to approve the repair, citing cost, timing, or a belief that the repair isn't necessary.
#### What You Do Right Now
Step 1: This is not your call to manage. The moment an owner refuses a habitability repair, escalate to the PM immediately. Do not continue the conversation with the owner as a coordinator.
Step 2: Document everything before you escalate:
- The nature of the repair and why it is a habitability issue
- The date and method of owner notification
- The owner's exact response: their words, in writing if possible
- The current condition of the unit and the resident's situation
Step 3: Notify the resident that the matter is being escalated and provide a realistic timeline. Do not tell the resident the owner refused. Say:
> "We are working on getting this resolved and I'll have an update for you by [specific time]."
#### What the PM Does With This
[LEGAL: in most states, the property management company has a legal obligation to maintain habitability regardless of owner approval — the PM must be the one to make the call on whether to proceed with the repair over the owner's objection, and must consult the broker and/or legal counsel before doing so. The coordinator never makes this call.]
#### What You Never Do
- Never tell a resident that the owner refused to approve a habitability repair
- Never close a habitability work order because the owner said no
- Never agree with an owner that a habitability repair "can wait"
- Never make promises to the resident about when the repair will happen until the PM has approved the timeline
- Never attempt to negotiate with the owner about a habitability repair; that is the PM's role
#### When It Goes to the PM
---
Critical: The moment the owner refuses. The coordinator's job ends at notification and documentation. Everything after that is the PM's responsibility.
#### What Gets Written Down
- Nature of the repair and the habitability basis for it
- Date and method of owner notification
- Owner's exact response: quoted directly if possible
- Date and time PM was notified
- What was communicated to the resident and when
- PM's decision and the basis for it
- Final resolution and timeline
### Scenario 6: A Resident Refusing Entry
#### What Happened
A vendor is scheduled for a repair or inspection. The resident refuses to allow entry, either by not being home, actively denying access, or communicating that they do not want anyone in the unit.
Common versions:
- Resident simply doesn't answer the door despite proper notice being given
- Resident calls to say they don't want the vendor in the unit while they're not home
- Resident says they never received notice of the scheduled entry
- Resident refuses entry for a non-emergency repair
- Resident refuses entry for a required inspection
#### What You Do Right Now
Step 1: Confirm proper notice was given. [LEGAL: most states require advance written notice before entry — typically 24–48 hours for non-emergency repairs; confirm your state's requirement with your broker. Emergency entry may not require advance notice.]
Step 2: If notice was properly given and the resident is refusing:
- Do not instruct the vendor to enter over the resident's objection
- Contact the resident to understand the reason for refusal
- Offer to reschedule if the reason is reasonable (resident at work, family emergency, etc.)
- Document the refusal and the reason given
Step 3: If the refusal is for a non-emergency repair:
- Reschedule with proper notice
- Document the refusal and the new scheduled date
- Notify the PM if this is a pattern or if the refusal is delaying a required repair
Step 4: If the refusal is for an emergency repair or a required inspection:
- Escalate to PM immediately
- [LEGAL: the PM must determine the appropriate legal response — which may include formal written notice of the right to enter, or in extreme cases, legal counsel — the coordinator never makes this call]
#### What You Never Do
- Never instruct a vendor to enter a unit over a resident's active objection
- Never enter a unit without proper notice except in a genuine emergency
- Never threaten a resident with lease consequences for refusing entry; that is the PM's role
- Never reschedule a habitability or emergency repair indefinitely because the resident keeps refusing; escalate to PM after the second refusal
- Never use a lockbox or master key to enter a unit when the resident has actively communicated they do not want entry: [LEGAL: unauthorized entry can create significant legal liability; consult your broker]
#### When It Goes to the PM
---
Critical: After the second refusal for any repair. Immediately for any refusal that involves a habitability issue, a required inspection, or a safety concern.
#### What Gets Written Down
- Date and method of original notice to resident
- Date and time of scheduled entry
- Resident's refusal: exact words if possible, method of communication
- Reason given by resident for refusal
- Whether the repair was rescheduled and the new date
- PM notification: time and response
- All subsequent contact with the resident regarding entry
### Scenario 7: Suspected Resident-Caused Damage on an Occupied Unit
#### What Happened
A vendor completes a repair and reports, or photos reveal, damage that appears to have been caused by the resident, not by normal wear and tear or a maintenance failure. The unit is still occupied. The resident may or may not be aware that the damage has been noticed.
Common versions:
- Vendor reports a hole in the wall that wasn't in the original work order
- Photos from a repair show pet damage, smoke staining, or unauthorized modifications
- A plumber reports a drain clogged with items that indicate resident misuse
- An HVAC tech reports filters that haven't been changed in years, causing system damage
- A vendor reports the unit is in a condition that suggests hoarding or neglect
#### What You Do Right Now
Step 1: Get documentation immediately:
- Photos and video from the vendor of everything they observed
- Vendor's written description of what they found
- Note the date, time, and vendor name in the work order
Step 2: Do not confront the resident. The coordinator does not accuse, charge, or communicate about suspected damage to the resident. That is the PM's role.
Step 3: Complete the original repair if it is within the approved scope. Do not withhold the repair as leverage.
Step 4: Escalate to PM with all documentation. The PM determines:
- Whether a mid-lease inspection is warranted
- Whether the resident should be charged for the damage
- Whether a lease violation notice is appropriate
- Whether the damage rises to the level of a lease termination issue
#### What You Never Do
- Never accuse a resident of causing damage, even if it is obvious
- Never tell a resident they will be charged for damage before the PM has reviewed and approved the charge
- Never withhold a repair because of suspected resident damage; complete the approved repair and escalate the damage separately
- Never share vendor observations about resident-caused damage with anyone other than the PM
- Never allow a vendor to discuss suspected damage directly with the resident; all resident communication goes through the coordinator or PM
- Never document suspected damage in a way that sounds like a conclusion; write what was observed, not who caused it
#### When It Goes to the PM
---
Critical: Immediately after documentation is collected. Any suspected resident-caused damage on an occupied unit is a PM-level decision on every step that follows. The coordinator's job ends at documentation and escalation.
#### What Gets Written Down
- Work order that prompted the vendor visit: original scope
- Vendor's name, date, and time on site
- Exact description of what the vendor observed: their words, not your interpretation
- Photos and video received and uploaded to the work order
- Whether the original repair was completed
- Time PM was notified and PM's response
- No communication to the resident logged until PM directs it
### Scenario 8: A Repeat Repair That Keeps Coming Back
#### What Happened
The same repair has been completed two or more times on the same unit or system, and the problem has returned again. The coordinator is about to dispatch for the third (or fourth) time. This is no longer a maintenance call. It is a pattern that signals either a vendor quality problem, a resident behavior problem, a systemic property issue, or all three.
Common versions:
- Drain keeps clogging after being cleared twice
- HVAC keeps failing after two service calls in the same season
- The same window keeps leaking after two sealing attempts
- A toilet keeps running after two repairs
- Pest treatment has been done twice and the infestation is back
#### What You Do Right Now
Step 1: Before dispatching again, flag the pattern. Pull the work order history and confirm:
- How many times has this repair been done?
- Who did the work each time?
- What was the diagnosis each time?
- What was the outcome each time?
Step 2: Do not dispatch the same vendor a third time for the same issue without PM review. A vendor who has failed to resolve the same problem twice has a performance problem that needs to be addressed, not rewarded with a third call.
Step 3: Escalate to PM with the full work order history. The PM determines:
- Whether a different vendor or specialist is needed
- Whether the root cause has been correctly identified
- Whether the issue is resident-caused (and whether a charge-back is appropriate)
- Whether the property needs a broader inspection to identify a systemic issue
- Whether the vendor should be held accountable for warranty or callback work
Step 4: Notify the resident that the pattern has been identified and that a different approach is being taken. Do not promise a specific outcome. Say:
> "We've identified that this has been an ongoing issue and we're bringing in [a specialist / a different approach] to get to the root cause."
#### What You Never Do
- Never dispatch the same vendor for the same failed repair a third time without PM review
- Never close a repeat work order without noting in the record that it is a recurrence
- Never tell a resident "we'll just keep fixing it"; that is not a resolution
- Never assume the resident is causing the problem without documentation
- Never let a repeat repair pattern go unescalated; three occurrences of the same issue is always a PM-level conversation
#### When It Goes to the PM
---
Critical: On the third occurrence of the same repair. On the second occurrence if the first repair was recent (within 30 days) or if the vendor's work quality is in question.
#### What Gets Written Down
- Full work order history: dates, vendors, diagnoses, outcomes
- Number of recurrences and the pattern identified
- Whether the same vendor was used each time
- Time PM was notified and PM's response
- Decision on vendor: same or replacement
- New diagnosis or specialist recommendation
- Communication to resident: time, method, content
### Scenario 9: Two Urgent Jobs and One Vendor
#### What Happened
Two urgent work orders come in at the same time, or nearly the same time, and you only have one available vendor for that trade in that area. You have to decide which job gets the vendor and which resident waits. This is the triage scenario, and guessing wrong means one resident gets a delayed response that may cross into a habitability or legal issue.
Common versions:
- Two no-heat calls on the same afternoon with one HVAC vendor available
- Two active leak calls with one plumber on the roster
- Two broken entry lock calls with one locksmith available
- One urgent and one emergency call, but the vendor is already dispatched to the urgent job
#### What You Do Right Now
Step 1: Assess both jobs against the emergency test from Scenario 1. If one is a true emergency and one is urgent, the emergency goes first. Always. No exceptions.
Step 2: If both are urgent (not emergency), triage using this priority order:
| Priority | Condition |
|---|---|
| 1st | Habitability impact: no heat, no water, active leak, security breach |
| 2nd | Vulnerable resident: elderly, disabled, family with infant, medical need |
| 3rd | Severity of property damage risk if delayed |
| 4th | Order in which the calls were received |
Step 3: Contact both residents immediately. The resident who is waiting deserves to know:
- That you are aware of their situation
- That you have a vendor scheduled and an estimated time
- That you will check in with them at a specific time
Step 4: Attempt to find a backup vendor for the second job. Check your emergency vendor list. Even a vendor who costs more is better than a resident waiting without a timeline.
Step 5: If you cannot find a second vendor and both jobs are urgent, escalate to PM. The PM makes the final triage call, not the coordinator.
#### What You Never Do
- Never triage based on which resident is easier to deal with or which owner is more demanding
- Never leave the second resident without contact and a timeline
- Never delay a true emergency because a vendor is already dispatched to an urgent job; call the vendor, explain, and redirect if necessary
- Never make the triage call alone when both jobs have habitability implications; escalate to PM
- Never promise the second resident a specific time you cannot guarantee
#### When It Goes to the PM
---
Critical: Any triage situation where both jobs have habitability implications, or where you cannot find a second vendor within one hour, goes to the PM for the final call.
#### What Gets Written Down
- Both work orders: time received, nature of issue, resident name and unit
- Triage decision: which job was prioritized and why
- Vendor dispatched to each job, or backup vendor search and outcome
- Time each resident was contacted and what they were told
- PM notification if escalated: time and response
- Resolution time for both jobs
### Scenario 10: A Vendor No-Show on a Scheduled Day
#### What Happened
A vendor was scheduled for a repair. The resident was notified and may have taken time off work or made arrangements to be home. The vendor does not show up and does not call. The resident contacts you, or you discover the no-show when following up.
#### What You Do Right Now
Step 1: Contact the vendor immediately. Call, text, and email. Give them 15 minutes to respond before moving to Step 2.
Step 2: If no response within 15 minutes:
- Begin identifying a backup vendor from your approved list
- Do not wait for the original vendor to call back before starting the backup search
Step 3: Contact the resident within 30 minutes of discovering the no-show:
> "I want to let you know that I'm aware your appointment didn't happen as scheduled and I sincerely apologize for the inconvenience. I'm working right now to get this rescheduled as quickly as possible and I'll have an update for you within the hour."
Step 4: If the repair is urgent or habitability-related:
- Escalate to PM immediately
- Dispatch backup vendor same day if at all possible
- Do not let a no-show on an urgent job result in a same-day delay without PM awareness
Step 5: If the repair is standard priority:
- Reschedule with the original vendor only if they have a documented reason and it will not happen again
- Consider whether the no-show warrants removing the vendor from your approved list; flag for PM review
Step 6: Document the no-show in the vendor's performance record.
#### What You Never Do
- Never tell the resident the vendor "must have gotten busy" or make excuses for the vendor
- Never reschedule with a no-show vendor for an urgent job without PM approval
- Never leave the resident without contact for more than 30 minutes after a confirmed no-show
- Never close the work order on the day of a no-show; it is not complete
- Never absorb a vendor no-show silently; it goes in the vendor's performance record every time
#### When It Goes to the PM
---
Critical: Immediately if the no-show involves an urgent or habitability repair. For standard repairs, notify the PM at end of day and flag the vendor performance issue. A vendor with two no-shows goes to the PM for removal consideration.
#### What Gets Written Down
- Scheduled date and time of the appointment
- Time the no-show was confirmed
- Attempts to contact the vendor: time, method, response or non-response
- Vendor's explanation if one was given
- Time resident was contacted and what was communicated
- Backup vendor search: outcome
- New scheduled date and time
- Vendor performance flag: noted in vendor record
- PM notification: time and response
### Scenario 11: An Invoice That Does Not Match the Quote
#### What Happened
A vendor submits an invoice after completing a job. The invoice amount is higher than the approved quote, sometimes slightly, sometimes significantly. The coordinator is being asked to approve payment or submit the invoice for processing.
Common versions:
- Invoice is $50 to $100 over quote with no explanation
- Invoice includes line items that were not in the original scope
- Invoice reflects an hourly rate that produces a higher total than the flat-rate quote
- Invoice includes a trip charge, disposal fee, or materials markup that was not disclosed upfront
- Invoice is for a completely different amount with no correlation to the quote
#### What You Do Right Now
Step 1: Do not approve or submit the invoice for payment. Hold it until the discrepancy is resolved.
Step 2: Compare the invoice line by line against the approved quote:
- What was quoted vs. what was invoiced
- Are there line items on the invoice that were not in the quote?
- Was the scope of work actually different from what was quoted? (If yes: was the change approved? See Scenario 3.)
Step 3: Contact the vendor for a written explanation of the discrepancy:
> "I'm reviewing your invoice against the approved quote and I'm seeing a difference of $[amount]. Can you send me a written explanation of the additional charges before I can process this for payment?"
Step 4: Assess the explanation:
| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| Vendor explains a scope change that was verbally approved mid-job | Check your records: was it approved? By whom? At what amount? If not documented, escalate to PM |
| Vendor added materials or fees not disclosed in the quote | Dispute the undisclosed charges; escalate to PM |
| Invoice reflects an honest billing error | Ask vendor to reissue a corrected invoice |
| Vendor insists the invoice is correct with no explanation | Escalate to PM immediately |
Step 5: Never pay a disputed invoice without PM approval.
#### What You Never Do
- Never approve an invoice that exceeds the quote without a documented explanation and PM approval
- Never pay an invoice to avoid a difficult conversation with a vendor
- Never tell a vendor "we'll just pay it this time"; this trains vendors to overbill
- Never process an invoice that includes unapproved scope items without PM review
- Never let invoice discrepancies go undocumented; every discrepancy goes in the vendor's performance record
#### When It Goes to the PM
---
Critical: Any invoice discrepancy that cannot be resolved with a corrected invoice from the vendor goes to the PM before payment is processed. Any invoice that comes in more than [FILL: your company's percentage-above-estimate flag, for example 10 to 15 percent] over the approved quote goes to the PM regardless of explanation. This is the same percentage-above-estimate flag captured in the maintenance setup questionnaire (Group B), so the invoice review and the board variance flag read one number.
#### What Gets Written Down
- Original approved quote: amount and scope
- Invoice received: amount, date, line items
- Discrepancy identified: specific differences
- Vendor contact: time, method, their explanation
- Resolution: corrected invoice, disputed amount, or PM escalation
- Final approved payment amount and who approved it
- Vendor performance note: flagged if explanation was inadequate
### Scenario 12: A Resident Threatening Code Enforcement or Legal Action
#### What Happened
A resident communicates, by phone, text, email, or portal message, that they intend to contact code enforcement, file a complaint with a housing authority, contact an attorney, or take legal action against the property management company or the owner. This may come as a direct threat or as a statement of intent.
Common versions:
- "If this isn't fixed by Friday, I'm calling the city."
- "I've already contacted a tenant's rights attorney."
- "I'm going to report this to the housing authority."
- "I'm withholding rent until this is repaired."
- "I'm going to sue you."
#### What You Do Right Now
Step 1: Do not react emotionally and do not argue. The coordinator's only job in this moment is to listen, acknowledge, and document.
Step 2: Acknowledge without admitting fault or making promises:
> "I hear you and I understand your frustration. I want to make sure this gets the attention it deserves. I'm going to escalate this to my manager right now and you'll hear back from us by [specific time today]."
Step 3: End the call or conversation professionally. Do not continue discussing the substance of the complaint; that is the PM's role.
Step 4: Escalate to PM immediately. Within 15 minutes. This is not a same-day escalation. This is a right-now escalation.
Step 5: Document everything before you do anything else:
- The resident's exact words: quoted directly
- The method of communication (call, text, email, portal)
- The date and time
- The underlying maintenance issue the resident is referencing
- The current status of any open work orders related to the complaint
Step 6: Do not contact the resident again until the PM has been briefed and has directed the response.
#### What You Never Do
- Never argue with a resident who is threatening legal action
- Never admit fault, apologize for the company's actions, or make any statement that could be construed as an admission of liability
- Never promise a specific repair timeline to a resident who has threatened legal action; that promise becomes a legal commitment
- Never tell a resident their threat "doesn't scare us" or any variation of that
- Never ignore a legal threat because you think the resident is bluffing
- Never respond to a resident's attorney in writing without PM and legal counsel direction
- Never delete or alter any communication record related to a legal threat: [LEGAL: document preservation is a legal obligation once litigation is threatened; consult your broker]

Critical: Immediately, within 15 minutes of the threat being made. No exceptions. The coordinator does not manage any aspect of a legal threat situation independently. The PM notifies the broker and legal counsel as appropriate.
#### What Gets Written Down

- Method of communication and timestamp

- Time PM was notified and PM's response

### The When-In-Doubt Rule
---
When you are not sure whether a decision is yours to make, it is not.
The maintenance coordinator seat has clear authority in three areas:
1. Dispatching approved vendors for approved work within the owner's authorization threshold
2. Communicating timelines and status updates
3. Documenting everything that happens, in real time, with photos and exact words
Everything outside those three areas is an escalation. When in doubt, apply this test in order:
#### The Four-Question When-In-Doubt Test
| Question | If YES | If NO |
|---|---|---|
| Question 1: Is anyone's safety at risk right now? | Dispatch emergency vendor immediately. Notify PM within 15 minutes. | Go to Question 2. |
| Question 2: Does this decision involve money above the owner's threshold? | Stop. Get written documentation. Escalate to PM before proceeding. | Go to Question 3. |
| Question 3: Does this decision involve a legal, habitability, or liability issue? | Stop all action. Escalate to PM immediately. Document everything first. | Go to Question 4. |
| Question 4: Have I done this exact situation before and do I know the outcome was right? | Proceed. Document as you go. Update PM at end of day. | Escalate to PM. Ask before acting. |
### The Three Things You Always Do, No Matter What
No matter which scenario you are in, no matter how much pressure you feel from a vendor, a resident, or an owner, these three actions are always correct:
| Always | Why |
|---|---|
| Document first: before you call anyone, before you dispatch anyone, write down what you know right now | Documentation is your protection in every dispute, every legal claim, and every PM conversation |
| Communicate to the resident: even if you have no answer yet, contact the resident with a timeline for when you will have one | A resident who feels heard and informed does not escalate; a resident left in silence does |
| Escalate to the PM: when in doubt, when the situation is new, when the stakes feel high | The PM seat exists to carry the decisions the coordinator seat was never designed to make alone |
### The Coordinator's Escalation Script
Use this exact language any time you need to pause a situation and escalate:
To a vendor pushing for an answer:
> "I hear you and I want to get you an answer fast. I need 15 minutes to get the right approval. Please hold where you are and don't proceed further. I'll call you back by [specific time]."
To a resident waiting for resolution:
> "I want you to know I'm actively working on this. I don't have a final answer for you yet, but I will by [specific time]. I'll contact you then regardless of where things stand."
To the PM when escalating:
> "I need your guidance on [situation]. Here's what I know: [facts]. Here's what I've done so far: [actions]. Here's what I need a decision on: [specific question]. I have [vendor / resident] waiting for a response by [time]."
